degradation in communication: ai-generated walls of text

24 june 2026

i came across the article "how to passive-aggressively shame people who use llms selfishly"1 by josh moody, where he defines selfish use of llms as a "situation in which someone uses an llm to save their own time at the expense of other people's time, resulting in a net productivity loss." and i have to say i do agree with the sentiment, and it made me reflect on my own time in academia to date. i am not the only one getting frustrated at this phenomenon. in both josh moody's article and the "no slop grenade"2 article they raise their frustrations and put forward methods (albeit silly) to tackle selfish ai usage, like using emoticons so the message isn't taken seriously, or simply sharing the latter article to call out the author.

currently i am working on a project which has come to a standstill. after trying to solve a problem by hand, provide a solution, run experiments, and write it up in less than 4 pages so my collaborators can work off it and give feedback, in return i get an ai-generated wall of text giving me "feedback" about the work. some of the ideas aren't present in the document or repo, suggesting that the sender didn't read either the document or the message they sent. clearly this is not only degrading research and collaboration but communication more generally.

this isn't the first time this has happened. in normal communication channels like teams, email and slack at work, it is extremely widespread. traditionally these mediums were quick and easily digestible, now plagued with walls of text where the sender hasn't even read or put thought into it. i understand using this for tedious tasks like responding to a landlord with correct grammar or punctuation, or using it to make sure your work is free of grammatical errors. but using this to answer simple questions, or in the case of a discussion with a peer, makes little sense. we outsource thinking to an llm and in turn expect the reader to decipher what is being said, when the sender never had any intention of thinking about the problem. this is made more evident in simple tasks: if someone asks you a yes/no question or what you'd personally prefer, we do not need to airbrush our responses to be "polite" or "correct".
