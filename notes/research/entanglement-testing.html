How can we test for entanglement in latent spaces?

1 June 2026

I have recently come across an interesting question: how can we steer our models in a certain direction, based on a metric of interest we want to optimise for, whilst not changing another? That is, how do we find a vector for our metric of interest which is not confounded with any other variable in our latent space? Can these vectors be found in a systematic way?

Formally, suppose $z \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the latent representation of our input $x$. We want to know whether two scalar properties $a,b$ are linearly decodable (i.e. there exists a direction $w$ such that $w^Tz \approx a$) from $z$ via independent directions $z = \alpha \mathbf{u}_a a + \beta \mathbf{u}_b b + \epsilon$, where $\mathbf{u}_a, \mathbf{u}_b \in \mathbb{R}^d$ are such that steering along $\mathbf{u}_a$ changes $a$ without affecting $b$, and vice versa. To test this, we simply learn $w$ where $a = w^Tz + b_0$ via linear regression, and $b_0$ is the bias term.

Recall that using MSE we wish to minimise the following loss: $L(w,b_0) = \mathbb{E}[(w^Tz + b_0 - a)^2]$. Let us begin by taking the derivative w.r.t. $b_0$: $\partial_{b_0} L = 2\mathbb{E}[w^Tz + b_0 - a] = 0$, so $b_0^* = \mathbb{E}[a] - w^T\mathbb{E}[z]$. Substituting this into our original formulation, we get $L(w,b_0^*) = \mathbb{E}[(w^Tz + \mathbb{E}[a] - w^T\mathbb{E}[z] - a)^2] = \mathbb{E}[(w^T( z - \mathbb{E}[z]) - (a - \mathbb{E}[a]))^2]$. Simply let $z = z - \mathbb{E}[z]$ and $a = a - \mathbb{E}[a]$ be mean centred, so $L(w) = \mathbb{E}[(w^Tz- a)^2]$. Let us now expand and rewrite: $$ \begin{align} \mathbb{E}[(w^Tz - a)^2] &= \mathbb{E}[(w^Tz)^2 -2w^Tza + a^2] \\ &= \mathbb{E}[(w^Tz)^2] - 2\mathbb{E}[w^Tza] + \mathbb{E}[a^2] \\ &= w^T\mathbb{E}[zz^T]w - 2w^T\mathbb{E}[za] + \text{Var}(a) \\ &= w^T\Sigma_z w - 2w^T\text{Cov}(z,a) + \text{Var}(a) \end{align} $$ Taking the derivative w.r.t. $w$: $\partial_{w} \mathbb{E}[(w^Tz- a)^2] = 2\Sigma_z w - 2\text{Cov}(z,a) = 0$, therefore $w^* = \Sigma_z^{-1}\text{Cov}(z,a)$, where $\Sigma_z = \mathbb{E}[zz^\top] \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is the covariance matrix of the representation $z$, capturing redundancy between dimensions of $z$, and $\text{Cov}(z,a) \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the vector of covariances between each dimension of $z$ and $a$, i.e. how each dimension of the latent space tracks the property $a$.

Going back to the original formulation ($z = \alpha \mathbf{u}_a a + \beta \mathbf{u}_b b + \epsilon$), we notice that $$ \begin{align} \text{Cov}(z,a) = \mathbb{E}[za] &= \mathbb{E}[(\alpha \mathbf{u}_a a + \beta \mathbf{u}_b b + \epsilon) a ] \\ &= \alpha \mathbf{u}_a \mathbb{E}[a^2] + \beta \mathbf{u}_b \mathbb{E}[ba] + \mathbb{E}[\epsilon a] \\ &= \alpha \mathbf{u}_a \text{Var}(a) + \beta \mathbf{u}_b \text{Cov}(b,a) \end{align} $$ where $\mathbb{E}[ba] = \text{Cov}(b,a)$, since both $b$ and $a$ are mean-centred, so $\text{Cov}(b,a) = \mathbb{E}[ba] - \mathbb{E}[b]\mathbb{E}[a] = \mathbb{E}[ba]$. The vectors $\mathbf{u}_a, \mathbf{u}_b \in \mathbb{R}^d$ factorise outside the expectation because they are fixed directions in latent space; they do not vary across sequences. Only $a$ and $b$ are random, so $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{u}_a a^2] = \mathbf{u}_a \mathbb{E}[a^2]$, exactly as a constant scales out of any expectation. As a result, the natural direction is $w^* = \Sigma_z^{-1}\text{Cov}(z,a) = \Sigma_z^{-1} (\alpha \mathbf{u}_a \text{Var}(a) + \beta \mathbf{u}_b \text{Cov}(b,a))$.

This is now the interesting part. We can construct a dataset such that $\text{Cov}(b,a) = 0$ by enforcing $p(b \mid a = \text{high}) = p(b \mid a = \text{low})$, i.e. $b$ is independent of $a$ by design. The contamination term vanishes: $$ \text{Cov}_{\text{matched}}(z,a) = \alpha \mathbf{u}_a \text{Var}_{\text{matched}}(a) + \beta \mathbf{u}_b \underbrace{\text{Cov}_{\text{matched}}(b,a)}_{=\,0} = \alpha \mathbf{u}_a \text{Var}_{\text{matched}}(a) $$ And therefore: $$ w^*_a = \Sigma_z^{-1} \alpha \mathbf{u}_a \text{Var}_{\text{matched}}(a) $$ which is only dependent on $\text{Var}(a)$, a scalar, meaning $w^*_a$ points purely along $\mathbf{u}_a$. Symmetrically, constructing a matched dataset where $\text{Cov}(a,b) = 0$: $$ w^*_b = \Sigma_z^{-1} \beta \mathbf{u}_b \text{Var}_{\text{matched}}(b) $$ We then test disentanglement via the dot product of the two cleaned probe directions: $$ w^{*\top}_a w^*_b \propto \mathbf{u}_a^\top \Sigma_z^{-2} \mathbf{u}_b \approx 0 \iff \mathbf{u}_a \perp \mathbf{u}_b $$ If this holds, $a$ and $b$ occupy independent directions in $z$ and can be steered separately. To quantify how well each probe works, we use $R^2$, the fraction of variance in $a$ explained by $w^*_a$ on held-out matched pairs: $$ R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_k (a^{(k)} - w^{*\top}_a z^{(k)})^2}{\sum_k (a^{(k)} - \bar{a})^2} $$ The numerator is the residual variance, what $w^*_a$ failed to explain. The denominator is the total variance in $a$. So $R^2 \in [0,1]$, where $R^2 = 1$ means $w^*_a$ perfectly predicts $a$ from $z$, and $R^2 = 0$ means no genuine direction for $a$ exists in $z$. Crucially, this is evaluated on held-out sequences from the matched dataset, sequences the probe never saw during training. Since the probe has never seen the test sequences, any predictive power must come from $w^*_a$ having found a genuine geometric direction in $z$ that generalises, not from memorising the training sequences. High $R^2$ on held-out sequences is therefore evidence that the direction $\mathbf{u}_a$ is a stable structure in the representation, not an artefact of fitting noise.

Note that probes can be trained independently at each layer $\ell$ of the model, giving a profile of $R^2_\ell$ across depth; disentanglement may emerge at some layers and not others. If $R^2_\ell \approx 0$ across all layers, no layer has learned a direction for $a$ independent of $b$, and steering along any $w^*_a$ will unavoidably also move $b$.
