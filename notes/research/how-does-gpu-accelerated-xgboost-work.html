let's recap how boosting works from a simple point of view, and what we're actually doing. we build shallow trees sequentially, each one fitted to the errors left over by the ensemble so far. Given a dataset $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ with $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$, the model is additive, so our model at step $m$, written $F_m$, is updated as $$ F_{m+1}(x_i) = F_m(x_i) + h_m(x_i) \approx y_i $$ writing the residual of the current model as $r_i^{(m)} = y_i - F_m(x_i)$, each new learner is fitted to those residuals: $$ h_m \;=\; \arg\min_{h \in \mathcal{F}} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(r_i^{(m)} - h(x_i)\big)^2 $$ so that $F_{m+1}$ corrects the errors of $F_m$. the update is approximate rather than exact because $h_m$ is drawn from a restricted class of shallow trees: it can only approximate the residual vector, never reproduce it. it's worth being precise about *why* the residual is the right target, rather than treating it as an obvious heuristic. take squared error as the per-example loss, and let $L$ be the empirical risk it sums to: $$ l\big(y_i, F(x_i)\big) = \tfrac12\big(y_i - F(x_i)\big)^2, \qquad L(F) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} l\big(y_i, F(x_i)\big) $$ since boosting updates predictions directly, we differentiate with respect to the prediction $F(x_i)$ itself rather than any model parameter: $$ \frac{\partial L}{\partial F(x_i)} \;=\; -\big(y_i - F(x_i)\big) \;=\; -\,r_i^{(m)} $$ so the residual is exactly the negative gradient of the loss with respect to the current prediction. fitting a tree to residuals is therefore not a heuristic, it is one step of steepest descent, taken in the space of predictions rather than the space of parameters.
